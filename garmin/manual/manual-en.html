Backyard

Your yard, your pace, and how long you'll get to rest.

A Garmin watch app for running a backyard ultra — any of them. It records your activity, anchors the bells to the top of the hour, and tells you, yard after yard, whether the pace you're holding will get you back in time to sit down. No server, no bib number, no connection needed.

Install

Connect IQ Store · free · 104 watches

Backyard installs like any other Connect IQ app, from your phone:

  1. Open the Connect IQ Store app (or apps.garmin.com in a browser).
  2. Search for Backyard and pick the listing «Backyard Ultra — lap, pace & rest».
  3. Tap Download and wait for the watch to sync.

It then shows up in the watch's activity list, next to Run and Bike — not among the widgets or watch faces. It is an activity like any other: you open it, press START, and the watch records.

Language

The app speaks English, Spanish, French, German, Italian and Portuguese, and picks one by itself from your watch's language setting. There is no language menu. If your watch is set to a language we don't carry, you get English.

Units

Kilometres or miles, whatever your watch says. The one exception is the yard distance setting, which is always written in kilometres even with the watch in miles — the setting's label says so. That's deliberate: if that one setting changed units, the factory 6.7 would mean 10.8 km of yard for half the world.

Settings

From the phone, or from the watch on the start line

Everything can be set in both places and lands in the same store, so it makes no difference which one you use. The usual thing is to get it ready from the phone back at the tent, and keep the watch for a last-minute change.

From the phone

In Garmin Connect: Devices → your watch → Connect IQ Apps → Backyard → Settings.

From the watch

On the start line (before you press START) or during the warm-up, hold the menu button — long UP on most Garmins — or, on touch watches, press and hold the screen. That opens «Yard settings».

Only before the first bell

Once the yard 1 bell rings, the menu stops opening. Changing the yard duration mid-race would move every bell after it, and the bell is not up for discussion.

Start time: Auto or set

With a set time — say 7:00 — the yard 1 bell is that time of day, no matter when you press START. Everything before it is warm-up. It's the safest option when you know your race's start time.

On Auto, the watch looks at when you press START and decides: if you've just passed an hour mark — within a grace period of one sixth of a yard, capped at 10 minutes — it takes the bell as already rung and puts you in yard 1. Otherwise it counts down to the next mark. Pressing at 8:03 leaves you running the 8:00 yard; pressing at 8:40 leaves you warming up until 9:00.

Choosing your screens

On the watch, Yard settings → Race screens opens a catalogue: every screen is drawn in full, with sample data, so you decide by looking rather than by guessing from a name. UP and DOWN walk through them, START shows or hides, BACK goes back.

The order — which one comes first — can only be changed from the phone: each screen has a position setting from 1 to 6, and 0 hides it. A watch face is no place to drag a list around.

Hide all of them and the app keeps the Yard screen: it never leaves you with nothing.

If you already had the app

Factory values only apply to fresh installs. Coming from an earlier version, your configuration is left exactly as it was — including «Auto yard at finish line» switched off and Margin sitting third. Change them by hand if you want the new defaults.

Running a race

From opening the app to the last bell you make

  1. Open the app and let it find GPS

    Open it from the activity list. The emblem fills the screen for five seconds — any button skips it — and behind it comes the start line: the yard you're about to run, with its distance and its minutes, and the state of the GPS.

    Open it early, back at the tent. The GPS starts searching the moment you do, and whatever it takes to lock is better spent there than on the line.

  2. Check what the screen says

    If the top of it doesn't show your loop's distance and your yard's minutes, now is when to fix it: long UP opens the settings. And wait for «GPS ready» in green at the bottom: while it says «Searching GPS» in grey, the watch still doesn't know where you are.

  3. Press START

    START starts the race without asking anything — with the race director counting down out loud, a confirmation dialog is in the way. From there on the watch is recording.

    If you pressed before the hour, you get the Warm-up screen counting down to your bell. It is not yard 1: in the activity file it stays in a lap of its own and never gets added to your first yard.

  4. Run the yard

    The bell rings — a long buzz and a tone — and for five seconds the screen announces the yard that's starting. After that you're in charge: UP and DOWN (or swiping up and down on touch watches) move through your screens.

  5. Mark it as you cross

    When you get in, press the lap button — LAP, bottom right on most Garmins — or let the automatic finish line do it for you. The screen turns green: you made it, go sit down. Below, the time to the next bell and what you've piled up so far.

  6. Get back to the line before the bell

    Three minutes out, the corral screen takes over whatever you were looking at. When the bell rings, the next yard opens by itself. No button opens a yard: the clock does.

  7. And when it's over, save

    START opens the finish menu. See Finishing and saving.

The six screens

In the order they come from the factory

Every screen has one big number. Twenty hours in, in the dark, with tired eyes, the only thing you actually read is whatever fills half the face; everything else is context for when you have time to spare, and in a backyard you never do.

Margin

The reason the app exists. The big number is the time you'll have left over — or be short by — to close the loop before the bell, at the pace you're holding. Green and you get to rest; red and you push.

The two rings say the same thing without a single digit: the outer grey one is the time used up in the yard, the inner coloured one the distance covered. When the inner ring overtakes the outer one, you're getting in before the bell.

Underneath: distance done against the target with your current pace, and what's left with what it's going to cost you.

Data per yard

The classic running grid, but about this yard only: distance covered since the bell, your pace in this yard, your heart rate right now, and the time left to the next bell.

Orange ring — orange is the colour of the yard throughout the app.

Total data

The whole-race twin, in blue. With one difference that matters: distance, pace and heart rate count only what you ran inside the yards — bell to mark — with the rests left out. That way the average pace is your running pace, not something watered down by sitting.

Time, on the other hand, adds up everything: yards and rests. It's how long you've been in the race.

Yard

How long until the next bell, with the ring emptying as the clock runs: what's left of the ring is what's left of the yard. Same number as the middle, readable without reading.

Below, which yard you're on and what you're doing: running or resting.

Total

The yards you've closed — the number that gets counted when this is over. The yard in progress joins the tally the moment you mark it, even though the hour hasn't closed yet.

Below, time in the race and the distance, worked out from the loop length.

Clock

Time of day and battery. In a thirty-hour race, knowing whether the battery will make it through the night is race information, not a luxury: under 20 % the percentage turns red, which is when you decide between changing the power mode and finding the charger.

The ones that take over

On top of the six there are four screens that show up without being asked and leave on their own. They can't be hidden: they're the ones you need to see.

The fourth is the starting notice: five seconds at every bell with the number of the yard beginning and a reminder of how long and how far it runs. Changing screen skips it.

The corral

The last three minutes of every yard

The corral isn't a screen you pick: it takes over. In the last three minutes of any countdown — the start included — it jumps on top of whatever you were looking at, because at that point what's urgent is getting to the line, not your pace. It leaves on its own when the bell rings.

3 min — yellow 2 min — orange 1 min — full red

At three and two minutes it's a wide coloured ring on black: you catch the colour at a glance with most of the screen switched off, which on an AMOLED is where the battery goes. Only the final minute floods red edge to edge, with the countdown in giant black. It's the most urgent warning and the shortest, and there the cost is worth it.

Running at night

Vibration and sound have their own switches in the settings. Sleeping next to your crew, you kill the sound and keep the buzz; want neither, kill both. The tone also obeys the watch's system sounds.

Marking the yard

What closes a yard, and what doesn't

One rule runs through the whole app, and it's worth getting straight: marking doesn't open the next yard. Marking says the yard ended — you got in, your rest starts now. The next one is opened by the clock, on its own, wherever the bell finds you.

Only the first mark of each yard counts. The rest are ignored without a sound, so one extra press breaks nothing.

The three ways to mark

The LAP button

The usual thing: you press as you cross. On most Garmins it's the bottom-right button.

Auto yard at finish line — on by default

The mark falls by itself when you come back within 30 metres of the start point: wider than a finish arch, narrower than the tent area. So it doesn't mark you on the way out — which is also «near the start» — it requires at least half a loop covered first.

The watch fixes that point when you start the race, and if the GPS hadn't locked yet, it picks it up at the first bell: at the top of the hour you're standing on the line by definition.

Auto yard by distance

The mark falls once the yard's distance is covered as your watch measures it. It can run alongside the finish-line one: whichever comes first does it.

Fully automatic

With the automatic marks on and LAP button off enabled, the button stops marking and there's no way to ruin a yard with a stray press. The automatic marks carry on as normal.

The target calibrates itself

Your watch might measure 6.85 km on a 6.7 km loop, and over thirty yards that gap throws the margin off. So every time you mark, the app keeps whatever it had measured — you're on the line, so that is the loop as this watch measures it — and uses it as the target for the next yard.

With a sanity filter: it only calibrates if what it measured looks like a yard, between 80 % and 120 % of the target. A loop you never finished doesn't move the reference.

How the margin works

The arithmetic the app is built on

The margin is a subtraction: the time left until the bell, minus how long it'll take you to close what's left of the loop at the pace you're holding.

paceyard time ÷ yard distance
to go(target − yard distance) × pace
margintime until the bell − to go

Positive is rest you can count on. Negative means you don't get in at that pace.

The pace shown isn't the pace multiplied

Three hundred metres in, your yard pace is still all over the place, and multiplying it by the six kilometres left would make the margin jump whole minutes from one stride to the next. The app used to handle that by keeping quiet: no margin until the first kilometre, which is eight minutes out of every hour without the number that matters most.

Now it leans on something better. When a yard opens, the best predictor of your pace isn't the yard — which has nothing in it — but what you've been running all day. So the projection mixes the two, weighted by the distance covered:

  • At metre zero, your average race pace rules.
  • At one kilometre, this yard's pace rules, and nothing else.
  • In between, the handover is continuous.

The pace you read on screen, though, is always this yard's raw pace: how you're going right now. The mix is only used for multiplying — the margin and the «to go X ≈ Y» line.

In yard 1 there's no average to lean on, so there the margin is the raw pace from the first minute, wobble and all. The first margin of the race is earned by running.

Why the dashes

Until the watch has enough distance to work out a pace — around a minute in — the margin and the pace show as --:--. There's nothing to calculate from, and a made-up number wearing that much confidence would be worse than a dash.

Finishing and saving

There are thirty hours recording underneath

START opens the finish menu, with three options:

ResumeCloses the menu and carries on. The way out with no consequences.
SaveCloses the activity, stores it on the watch and uploads it to Garmin Connect like any other race.
DiscardThrows the activity away. It asks for confirmation — the only option in the menu that does.

During the race BACK does nothing, deliberately: there's an activity recording underneath and a backyard doesn't offer second starts. This menu is the only door.

What lands in Garmin Connect

An ordinary running activity, with its laps: every bell closes one lap and opens the next, so the file's laps are your yards, hour by hour. The warm-up — whatever you ran or waited through before your first bell — sits in a lap of its own and never gets added to yard 1.

It syncs to Garmin Connect by itself, and on to Strava or wherever else you have connected, like any other activity.

If the watch dies

Flat battery, a reboot, an accidental exit

Over eighty or a hundred hours it happens. The app saves what matters at every bell and every mark, so when you open it again it asks:

ResumeCarries on where the race was. Since the anchor is the clock, the yard count comes back exact even if the watch was off for a while.
New raceThrows away the saved one and takes you to the start line.

Resuming opens a new recording: Connect IQ won't let the old one continue, so in Garmin Connect you'll see the race split into two activities. The race count, though, stays exact — it's driven by the wall clock, not by the stopwatch.

You have to answer: this question can't be skipped with BACK.

Common confusions

What tends to throw people

The margin shows --:-- and won't budge

Not enough distance yet. For the first long minute of every yard there's nothing to work a pace out of. If it's still like that several minutes in, the watch isn't measuring distance: check that the GPS locked.

The automatic finish line never marks

Check that Auto yard at finish line is on in the settings, and that the GPS had locked. It also needs at least half a loop covered before it marks, so it doesn't catch you on the way out.

I marked the yard and the next one hasn't started

That's right: marking closes the yard, it doesn't open the next one. The bell opens the next one, at the top of the hour. Until then you're resting — that's the green screen.

I pressed START late and I think everything's off

It isn't. The yard 1 bell anchors to the top of the hour, not to the moment you press. Starting at 8:03 leaves you running the 8:00 to 9:00 yard, with its bell nailed to 9:00. And if you'd set a fixed start time, there was nothing to work out in the first place.

The settings won't open

They only open before the yard 1 bell — on the start line or during the warm-up. After that they close on purpose: changing the yard duration mid-race would move every bell after it.

The watch distance doesn't match the loop

That's normal, and the app is built for it: it calibrates the target with what your watch measures every time you mark a yard. Let the first yard go by and you'll see the margin settle itself.

I can't find the app on the watch

It isn't a widget or a watch face: it's in the activity list, with Run and Bike. If it isn't there either, sync the watch with your phone again.

Before your first race

Try it on a training run with the yard set to 5 or 10 minutes and a short distance. In one go you see the bell, the corral, the mark and the rest, and you turn up to the real start line knowing what's about to happen. A backyard is no place to find out how an app works.
